Can a small, fast judgment model check an agent's code after every edit, against rules no deterministic linter can express, and does the agent get better for it?     Entry 8 · A local model close to Jev's accuracy, but not its speed 28 Sep – 1 Oct 2026

    Question. Entry 7's fine-tuned Kev-4B was promising, but it learned from two packs only and was slow. Can retraining it on every labelled edit bring it close to Jev? And can it then answer a hook check on this Mac as fast as cloud Jev?

    How it was run. As three autoresearch rounds: an agent tries one change at a time and keeps it only if it clears a bar written down before the first experiment. Every experiment is logged, including the ones that failed. The full log is published separately: autoresearch log.

    Local vs Jev

    {{E8_SUMMARY}}

    Accuracy is scored the same way for all three, on the same 2,089 held-out edits across all five packs. Jev's row reuses its cached Entry 7 judgments, rescored for this entry. A few rules were reworded since those judgments, and there's no committed result file for this row. Jev's times are the cloud API's live numbers, not this workload's. Its cost is an estimate: $1.51 per 10,000 edits measured before the new packs, plus about 29% more input tokens with them.

    • Close to Jev on accuracy, but not equal. The best local setup scores {{E8_E22_F1R}}% held-out F1 against Jev's {{E8_JEV_F1}}%. It mostly misses more (recall {{E8_E22_R}}%), and it flags {{E8_E22_FAR}} of clean edits against Jev's {{E8_JEV_FA}}.
    • Slower than Jev. {{E8_E22_MS}} ms median per check against Jev's 343 ms, and {{E8_E22_P90}} ms at the 90th percentile.
    • But free and offline. There's no per-check cost, and no code leaves the machine. It needs about 20 GB of memory for three model servers.

    Retraining Kev-4B (rounds 1 and 2)

    Setup. We used every labelled dev edit, for all packs and languages: 2,628 cases. A hash of each case id splits them three ways:

    • train (1,835 cases): the model learns from these;
    • calibration (282): used only to fit the model's temperature, which sets how sharp its probabilities are;
    • TUNE (511): never trained on. Every keep-or-discard decision was made here.

    The held-out edits (HOLDOUT, 2,089 cases) were scored only twice: for the round-1 baseline and for the final best. Labels are ours, never Jev outputs. Training ran on a DGX Spark.

    Rules, fixed before the first experiment. Each experiment changes one thing. It's kept only if TUNE F1 rises by at least 1.0 point, with at most 5% of clean edits flagged. A change meant to cut training cost may be kept if it loses at most 1.0 point. Promising results are trained again with a second random seed and judged on the mean.

    Round 1 retrained Entry 7's recipe on all of that data. It scored TUNE F1 94.53, and HOLDOUT F1 {{E8_B_F1}} with {{E8_B_FA}} of clean edits flagged. Training took {{E8_TB}} h.

    Round 2 ran {{E8_N2}} experiments and kept {{E8_KEPT2}}.

    {{CHART_R2}}

    TUNE F1 per experiment. Two dots joined by a line are two seeds of the same change. The incumbent drops after e04 because e04 was kept under the cost rule.

    • e04: train only on the questions the hook asks. Training only on the rules the gate would ask cost about 1 point (93.55, mean of two seeds). But it made training {{E8_GATE_X}}× cheaper ({{E8_TB}} h → {{E8_TG}} h), so it was kept under the cost rule. That made the rest of the round affordable.
    • e12: one extra copy of each example, under another project folder. Nearly every labelled edit lives under /repo/. Adding one copy of each under a different folder, with the same file and rules, scored 95.48 and 95.26 on two seeds. That's +1.8 over e04, and the only clear win.
    • About half of e12's gain is just more training. e12 doubles the records. A control with plain duplicates (e15) recovered about half the gain (+1.05 against +1.82). It ran with one seed, so that split is uncertain.
    • Seed noise is as large as the effects. Two trainings that differed only in their random seed scored up to {{E8_SPREAD}} points apart. That's why every promising result was trained twice.
    • No gain from: 1 or 3 epochs, a higher learning rate, a separate learning rate for the output head, focal loss, a Brier loss term, extra copies of the violations, extra look-alike clean edits (alone or on top of e12), two folder copies, or a lower flag threshold (0.35).

    On held-out edits, e12 beats the baseline. HOLDOUT F1 rose from {{E8_B_F1}} to {{E8_E12_F1}}, and clean edits flagged fell from {{E8_B_FA}} to {{E8_E12_FA}}. It also trained in {{E8_TE12}} h instead of {{E8_TB}} h. Served on the Mac (MLX) instead of the Spark, the same model scores {{E8_E12M_F1}} with {{E8_E12M_FA}} flagged; the difference is two hygiene cases.

    HOLDOUT by pack: F1 (clean edits flagged){{E8_PACKS}}

    From the baseline to e12, security improved most (96.0 → 97.6), while hygiene and performance dipped slightly. Each pack has between 65 and 353 held-out violations, so changes under a point are within noise.

    Making it fast (round 3)

    Setup. One check is what the hook does for one edit: look up the rules, apply the gate, and send one request per file to the local server over a warm connection. The workload is 100 fixed checks from TUNE. A trial runs all 100. Each experiment ran 2 warm-up trials and 5 measured ones, and its result is the median of the 5 trial medians. The Mac is an M3 Max with 64 GB.

    • Targets: 343 ms, cloud Jev's live median, and a stretch target of 230 ms, Jev with a warm connection.
    • Accuracy gate: on a fixed 400-case subset of TUNE, F1 at most 1.5 points below e12's (95.48, so at least 93.98), at most 5% of clean edits flagged, and no errors. Also, the "fix" tier (p ≥ 0.8) must stay at least as precise as all flags (p ≥ 0.5).
    • Keep rule: at least 5% faster than the best so far, the gate passes, and even the slowest trial beats the best's median.

    Baseline: e12 as served took {{E8_R3_BASE}} ms median per check, and {{E8_R3_BASE_P90}} ms at the 90th percentile.

    {{CHART_R3}}

    Median per check, log scale. Hover a point for its accuracy. The best-so-far line uses each kept change's own median. e22's {{E8_E22_MS}} ms comes from the interleaved run described below.

    What worked, in the order it was kept:

    1. Less padding (e03, 1,607 ms). A batch pads every rule's text to the longest one, and 38% of the tokens were padding. Grouping rules by length removed most of it, with the same answers.
    2. A rule-first model with a second opinion (e13, 734 ms). Normally the model reads the code, then each rule's long text. In the rule-first layout, each rule's text is processed once at startup and kept. A check then only runs the code once per rule, all in one batch. That's 2.8× faster at the same model size. But the model must be trained for that layout, and it's less accurate. So a small 2B rule-first model answers first, and any rule it scores between 0.1 and 0.9 is asked again on e12. We call this a cascade.
    3. Cheaper second asks (e16, 648 ms). They usually cover 1–2 rules, so they now run in one pass instead of two.
    4. A three-stage chain (e22, {{E8_E22_MS}} ms). The 2B rule-first model answers first. Rules it's unsure of (0.1–0.9) go to a rule-first 4B, and rules the 4B is unsure of go to e12. Measured in 5 pairs, interleaved with e16, it was faster in all 5: {{E8_E22_MS}} ms against {{E8_E16_PAIRED}} ms.

    The best setup, e22, takes {{E8_E22_MS}} ms median and {{E8_E22_P90}} ms at the 90th percentile, 77% faster than the baseline. On HOLDOUT it scores {{E8_E22_F1}} F1 (precision {{E8_E22_P}}%, recall {{E8_E22_R}}%). It flags {{E8_E22_FA}} of clean edits, and {{E8_E22_P8}}% of its "fix" findings are real. That's 0.5 points below e12 alone, with fewer false alarms but lower recall. Security drops most, from 97.6 to 93.2.

    Neither target was met by anything that passed the gate. The fastest setups all failed it:

    • Rule-id 4B (e20): 245 ms, F1 72.4. It reads the code once and sees each rule only as its id. Without the rule text, it can't judge the rules.
    • Rule-first 0.8B (e04): 118 ms, F1 71.7.
    • 0.8B in the normal layout (e02): 327 ms, F1 75.6. It's simply a weaker model.

    What didn't help:

    • 8-bit and 4-bit weights. 8-bit was no faster, and 4-bit was slower and less accurate. On Apple's GPU, reading the prompt is limited by compute, not by loading weights.
    • Wiring memory, which keeps a model's buffers from being paged out, for one server or both. No gain.
    • Running the servers at the highest scheduling priority (QoS): 3.7% faster, under the 5% bar.
    • Dropping each rule's "false" criterion at serve time: 18% faster, but F1 fell to 89.4 and 9% of clean edits were flagged.
    • A third training epoch for the 2B student: no more accurate on the calibration split, and worse calibrated.
    • Narrower second-ask bands, including one fitted on the calibration split: all failed the gate.

    The Neural Engine. We converted one Kev-4B layer's matrix multiplies to Core ML. On the Neural Engine they ran at about 5 TFLOPS, slower than the GPU's ~11 (10.4 ms against 6.6 ms at 256 tokens). The probe left out the layer's DeltaNet scan, a step-by-step recurrence, because Core ML has no operation for it; it would need a chunked rewrite. So the Neural Engine isn't a way to speed up this model.

    Where the time goes. The 4B is compute-bound on the GPU at about 1,000 tokens per second. A normal-layout check is about 1,200 tokens, mostly rule text and padding; the code itself is a median of 115. Cost grows with rules asked: about 90 ms for 1 rule, 720 ms for 5, and 1.3–2.3 s for 10 or more (e16).

    Rules changed during the round. These are changes to the contract, made after some results were in:

    • Extension rule (after e03). When a candidate cleared the 5% bar but one trial broke the "slowest trial" check, 5 more trials ran, and the decision used all 10, allowing 1 slow trial in 10. e03, e16, e19 and e21 were judged this way.
    • A2 (after e12, post hoc). At about 96% precision, the "fix tier at least as precise" clause flipped on a single pair. From e13 on, the fix tier may be up to 1.0 point less precise. e11 and e12 keep their "gate failed" result. Under A2 both would have passed, and e11 (1,080 ms) would have been kept; later setups were faster anyway.
    • A3 (after e21). Back-to-back trials of the same setup swung between 544 and 925 ms within minutes. From e22 on, a candidate is measured interleaved with the incumbent in 5 pairs. e22 is e21's chain re-tested this way. Under the old rule, e21 was discarded (541 ms, with 2 of 10 trials slow).
    • The calibration clause wasn't checked by the script until e11. It was fixed then, and the baseline and e03 were re-checked; both pass.

    Caveats.

    • The Mac was busy and swapping, with up to about 30 GB of swap in use, so absolute times are noisy.
    • Latency wasn't measured through the hook's real daemon. It used the hook's client code in one bun process. HOLDOUT latency wasn't measured either.
    • The chain needs about 20 GB of memory for its three servers. That's an estimate, not a measurement. The servers are stopped now.
    • The round stopped after {{E8_N3}} of 30 planned experiments. The remaining ideas needed new multi-hour trainings on the Spark, and renders held the GPUs for about 10 hours of this round. An offline simulator on cached judgments ruled out the cheap variants first: narrower bands, a 0.8B front stage, and rule-id first stages.
    • A rule-first 4B was also trained for 3 epochs, but its result isn't in the ledger.

    GPU priority

    Another project's render jobs share these GPUs, and a render always gets the GPU first. Before every job, a gate checked for renders and waited. It didn't always work:

    • Rounds 1–2: a training run held the Spark GPU into a render. The gate only ran before a job starts. A render began at 13:09 UTC on 29 Sep while e12's second seed was training, and our run kept the GPU for about 1.5 h of it. After that, every job ran with a watchdog that stops it within about a minute of a render starting.
    • Then the watchdog had a bug. It matched its own command line, so it never exited. The GPU sat idle for about 4 h when it could have been used. It was fixed and self-tested.
    • Rounds 1–2 waited about 11.5 h for renders in total.
    • Round 3: about 595 min of logged Mac waits, and the watchdog stopped 9 Spark trainings for renders or other GPU work. Once, a queue-helper bug ran two of our own training jobs together for about 2 min before one was stopped by hand.

    What we learned

    1. Fine-tuning works with more data. One Kev-4B model now covers all five packs at about 95% held-out F1. Jev still leads by 3–4 points, with fewer false alarms.
    2. Data changes beat training settings. The only real gain came from a simple data change: the extra copy under another folder. Every loss, learning rate and epoch change was flat or worse.
    3. Speed is a model-shape problem. The 4B is compute-bound. The large wins came from changing what the model reads (rule-first) and asking a bigger model only when the small one is unsure. Serving tweaks, quantization and the Neural Engine didn't help.
    4. Matching Jev's speed locally needs a different architecture, not more tuning. The models fast enough to beat 343 ms all lost 20 or more F1 points.
    5. On a busy machine, measure interleaved. Sequential trials of one setup varied by almost 2× within minutes.

    How the rules were made. The packs follow one workflow:

    1. Research. 120 rules, each with its cited evidence in rules/sources/.

    Fine-tuned Kev-4B

    Setup. We followed the Entry 5 plan. Kev-4B got a LoRA fine-tune (a small set of trainable weights added to the frozen model) on the tuning split only. The held-out split was never exported, so the model can't have seen it.     JevLike

    Question. Is JevLike better trained than Kev or Laya for this?

    What it is. It's a training kit rather than a pretrained judge. It provides an option-attention scoring head, which you train on your own labeled data over either a byte encoder learned from scratch or a frozen Hugging Face encoder.     • Human review. Before review, the hook halves the rule-covered comments a reviewer would otherwise write (sync: 0.96 → 0.50 per task). At about $0.04 per task, it's cheap against even one minute of a reviewer's time.

    Real agent runs

    Claude Code (Sonnet) did 12 general tasks, such as a GitHub client, a rate limiter and a disk cache. Each ran twice under three conditions: no hook, both tiers, and high tier only. GPT-5.5 graded the final files blind.

    • The agent acted on the feedback. It fixed 19 of 20 high-tier findings, and mechanical violations went to near zero. For example, Swift force-unwraps fell from 6 to 1.
    • The overall effect was small. Violations per task went from 2.29 to 1.83 with both tiers, and to 1.71 with the high tier only. The 95% interval includes zero.
    • Why. The agent already wrote fairly clean code, and most of what remained were judgment calls on names and numbers.

    These numbers used the hygiene pack only. The full v1 page is kept at report/archive/2026-09-26-v1-hygiene-report.html.

    Jev vs the grader on real code

    On 98 real agent-written files, Jev and GPT-5.5 agreed on mechanical rules. They split on vague names and magic numbers. Mostly that was the grader reading the rule literally: let (data, _) = … fails because data is on the banned list, and defaulted option values count as magic numbers. Jev's one clear miss was four-level nesting.

    Recommendation

    Entry 9 adds optional Cloudflare Clef support. Keep Jev as the measured default until the hosted comparison is completed.

    1. Install the hook with the default packs on: hygiene, practices, security, tests and performance ({{LIVE_ALL}} rules). Candidates stay off until they pass. Use it in Claude Code and Codex, as a non-blocking hint with thresholds 0.8 / 0.5, and pin JEV_LINT_MODEL=jev-1.13.0. (Until Entry 7 this said "both packs"; the new packs cost almost no time on real code.)
    2. Match the setup to your review process. It's best value with human review, or where no AI reviewer checks every change. There, run the hook async and always on. If an AI reviewer plus a fix round already run on every task, the hook is marginal (Entry 6). Consider a review-time Jev check instead, which is cheaper and untested.
    3. Leave the warm daemon on (the default). It saves about 30 ms per check on a quiet machine and more under load. JEV_LINT_DAEMON=off checks in-process.
    4. Keep the gate on (the default). It skips about two thirds of rule checks per edit at no measured recall cost.
    5. In Claude Code, run it async: asyncRewake: true with JEV_LINT_MODE=rewake. It's as effective as synchronous and saves about 6 s per task. Entry 7's run with every pack on used async too, and cut violations significantly. In Codex, keep it synchronous, since Codex has no rewake.
    6. Let each repo learn from the hook. The findings log records what fired and whether the agent fixed or kept it. Run the jev-lint-learn skill weekly on active repos: it turns recurring fixes into AGENTS.md guidance, and repeatedly ignored flags into rule rewrites. In its first real use, it narrowed this repo's own stdout rule after a false positive.
    7. Keep cloud Jev as the judge. For offline or zero-cost use, the local chain from Entry 8 is a working fallback: about 0.5 s per check and {{E8_E22_F1R}}% held-out F1, against Jev's {{E8_JEV_F1}}%. Point TYPESAFE_BASE_URL at it. Expect more missed violations than with Jev, and about 20 GB of memory in use. (New in Entry 8.)
    8. Keep the AI code reviewer. The hook handles the pattern-level issues cheaply before review, so review time goes to logic and design, which is where about 75% of review findings were.
    9. Treat rule wording like code. Before adding a rule, add labeled examples and hard negatives, tune on the dev split, and accept it only if the held-out split holds up. The harness does this in one command.
    10. Consider the high tier only for practice rules if noise shows up in practice. Their double-check band was 64% real, compared with 86% for hygiene rules.
    11. Move nesting depth and floating promises to ESLint/SwiftLint. Jev is weak at counting and tracing.

    Methods

    • Jev: jev-latest (jev-1.13.0), one call per edit, one yes/no question (a Noul) per rule. $0.042 per million input tokens; output tokens are free.
    • LLM judge, grader, reviewer and mapper: GPT-6-Luna at reasoning_effort: low. $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output, standard tier, checked 26 Sep 2026. Entry 1 originally used GPT-5.4-mini and GPT-5.5.
    • Codex (Entry 5): codex-cli 0.157.1 (Homebrew), gpt-6-astra at medium reasoning (the user's default), codex exec with an isolated CODEX_HOME per run and --dangerously-bypass-hook-trust.
    • Local models (Entry 4): Kev-0.8B and Kev-4B (jaredpalmer/kev-*, MLX, bf16); Laya 0.3.20 English checkpoint (PyTorch Metal backend; ONNX Runtime 1.30 with the Core ML provider). Held-out split only, one request at a time, on an M3 Max with 64 GB.
    • Agent: Claude Code headless (Sonnet). User hooks, plugins and MCP servers were off. Hook conditions were interleaved.
    • Labels: written by Claude subagents, one labeler per edit. Tuning and held-out sets come from different authors. Thresholds were fixed at 0.8 / 0.5 before any data came in.
    • Rule packs (Entry 7): each rule is researched, with its evidence in rules/sources/. It gets labelled dev and held-out edits from separate authors, with hard negatives, and is reworded on dev only. It then enters the pack as a candidate, which the hook skips.
    • Shipping bar: the src/validate.ts bar, fixed before the new packs were measured. On held-out edits, with the gate on: precision of at least 90% at p ≥ 0.8; precision of at least 75% and recall of at least 80% at p ≥ 0.5; and at least 3 held-out violations. eval/pack-status.ts applies it and records per-rule numbers in eval/results/pack-status.json. Passing rules lose the candidate status. A rule can still be held back on real-code evidence, as ts-perf-linear-lookup-in-loop was.
    • Kev fine-tune (Entry 7): a LoRA on Kev-4B, trained on 541 tuning-split records. The temperature was fitted on 85 held-back tuning records. The held-out split was never exported.
    • Kev retraining (Entry 8): Kev-4B warm-started from jaredpalmer/kev-4b, LoRA, 2 epochs, learning rate 2e-5, 2,000 replay records from Kev's own data, bf16 backbone weights, on a DGX Spark (GB10). Splits by sha256(case id) % 100: TUNE 0–19, calibration 20–31, train 32–99. Scoring is local/autoresearch/score.ts: the case's own pack, shipped rules only, gated, flagged at p ≥ 0.5, and only rules in the case's label scope. The ledgers and per-experiment notes are in local/autoresearch/; the ledgers themselves aren't committed, so report/build.py keeps a copy of the numbers and checks it against them when they exist.
    • Latency (Entry 8): 100 fixed TUNE checks per trial with the hook's client code in one bun process, 2 warm-up and 5 measured trials, decided on the median of the trial medians. From e22 on, interleaved A/B in 5 pairs. Kev servers on MLX (bf16) on an M3 Max with 64 GB. Trials that overlapped a render were discarded and rerun.
    • Jev on Entry 8's scoring: Jev's cached Entry 7 judgments for the 2,089 held-out edits, rescored with score.ts's scoring. Most were judged before a few later rule rewordings.
    • Skill eval (Entry 7): 5 private repos with hand-written convention lists, 2 headless Claude Code runs per repo and version, a 30-minute cap per run. Luna (low) judged, by majority of 3 votes. Only anonymised totals are published.
    • Caveats:
      • The labels are synthetic.
      • Luna is both the competitor and the grader, so shared blind spots could favour it.
      • The end-to-end samples are small: 24 runs per condition.
      • Several Entry 7 timings (the real-file rule-set comparison and the daemon) were measured by hand under varying machine load, and have no committed result file.
      • Entry 8's latencies were measured on a busy, swapping Mac, and its round-3 contract changed three times during the round (see Entry 8).
